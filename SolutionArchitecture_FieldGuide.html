Field Guide · Solution Architect Track

Solution Architecture Field Guide

You do not need to become a full-time solution architect. You need the ~20% of the discipline that lets you direct an AI system toward the right design, evaluate a design an AI (or a vendor, or a team) hands you, and approve or reject it with defensible reasons. This guide teaches that 20%, then shows you what it produces on a real initiative, end to end.

How to use this guide

Read the Discipline section once, in order. Then use the Worked Example and the eight Documents as your reference set the next time an AI system (Claude, an architecture copilot, a vendor SOW) hands you a design and asks for your sign-off.

The worked example

Meridian Mutual Insurance, a mid-size Property & Casualty carrier, is building ClaimSight - an AI-powered claims intake, triage, and fraud-risk platform. Requirements are already approved; every document below is built against that one real, believable initiative.

What "approved" means here

Discipline · 01

Why Solution Architecture Exists

Every system will eventually be built one way or another. Solution architecture exists to make sure it is built the right way on purpose, instead of the accidental way that emerges from whoever wrote code first.

A solution architect's job is to sit between "what the business needs" (requirements) and "what gets built" (code, infrastructure, integrations) and make a small number of high-leverage decisions early, deliberately, and in writing - decisions that are expensive to reverse once concrete is poured. Naming a variable is cheap to change. Picking synchronous request/response instead of an event backbone, when your real load pattern is a 46x spike during a hurricane, is not.

Without architecture

Every developer (or every AI coding session) makes local decisions that look correct in isolation. Six months later the system has three different ways of calling the same downstream API, no consistent error handling, and nobody can say what happens when the mainframe is slow.

With architecture

A small set of named, written, justified decisions (ADRs) constrain the space of "reasonable next steps" so that a new engineer, a new AI session, or a new vendor converges on a consistent system instead of reinventing structure every sprint.

Why this matters more, not less, in an AI-generated codebase

An AI coding agent is extremely good at producing plausible, locally-correct code quickly - and extremely bad at knowing, on its own, whether a 46x Catastrophe-event traffic spike is a normal Tuesday or a once-a-decade edge case for your business. It will not push back on an architecture that "works in the demo." That judgment call is yours. The discipline in this guide is what you use to direct the AI before it builds, and to evaluate what it hands back.

The Architect's Core Job in One Sentence

Convert business intent into a small number of durable, written, testable decisions - then hold every subsequent design (AI-generated or human) accountable to them.

Discipline · 02

Quality Attributes & Non-Functional Requirements

Functional requirements describe what the system does. Quality attributes (also called non-functional requirements, or NFRs) describe how well it does it - and they are what actually determine whether an architecture is fit for purpose. A system that does the right thing but falls over during your busiest week has a functional pass and an architectural failure.

The rule an architect enforces

Every quality attribute must have a measurable target, not an adjective. "Fast" is not a requirement. "p95 API latency under 800ms at 1x average load" is. If an AI-generated design document uses words like "highly scalable" or "secure by design" without a number attached, send it back.

Core quality attribute categories an architect must interrogate
CategoryDefinitionTypical measurable target pattern
PerformanceSpeed of response under expected loadp50/p95/p99 latency in ms, throughput in requests/sec
ScalabilityBehavior as load grows, including sudden spikesMax multiple of average load sustained, time-to-scale (elastic response time)
AvailabilityProportion of time the system is usable99.9% = ~8.7 hrs downtime/year; stated per environment/time window
Reliability / DRCorrectness over time and recovery from failureRecovery Time Objective (RTO), Recovery Point Objective (RPO)
SecurityProtection of data and access from misuseEncryption standard, auth model, audit coverage %, time-to-patch
ComplianceAdherence to regulation and policyNamed framework (SOC 2, HIPAA, NAIC, GDPR), audit pass rate
MaintainabilityEase of understanding and safely changing the systemLead time for a change, file/function size ceilings, test coverage
UsabilityFitness of the system for the humans who operate itTask completion time, error rate, training time
Cost efficiencyEconomic sustainability of running the systemCost per transaction, cost per user, infra spend vs. budget
ObservabilityAbility to know what the system is doing right nowMTTR, % of services with structured logs/correlation IDs

Where NFRs come from

NFRs are not invented by the architect in a vacuum. They are derived from: (1) the business requirement itself (a 15-minute FNOL target implies a latency budget), (2) known operational patterns (a Catastrophe weather event implies a load-spike NFR), (3) regulatory obligation (a state Department of Insurance implies data-residency and retention NFRs), and (4) organizational risk appetite (how much downtime leadership will actually tolerate). See the full worked NFR Specification in Document 4 for 20 concrete, measurable targets derived this way for ClaimSight.

Discipline · 03

Architecture Styles: Monolith vs. Services vs. Event-Driven

The architecture style is the load-bearing structural choice everything else hangs off. Getting it wrong is expensive to undo. Getting it "maximally modern" when you did not need it is just as wrong as getting it too simple.

Three architecture styles at a glance. ClaimSight uses event-driven (rightmost) at its core, with synchronous REST only at the two edges (portal, workbench) - see ADR-001.
When each style earns its place
StyleStrong fit when...Weak fit when...
MonolithSmall team, low/steady load, fast time-to-market matters more than independent scalingDifferent parts of the system need to scale independently, or need independent release cycles
Service-Oriented (sync)Clear domain boundaries, moderate load, request/response fits the interaction naturallyLoad is bursty/unpredictable; a slow downstream service blocks the whole chain
Event-DrivenElastic scale needed (spiky load), multiple independent consumers of the same fact, durable audit/replay requiredSimple CRUD app, small team unfamiliar with eventual consistency, over-engineering risk on day one

Over-engineering is a real failure mode

Event-driven architecture is not "more advanced," it is a trade: you gain elasticity and durability, you pay in operational complexity and eventual consistency. An architect's job includes rejecting event-driven design for a system with no spike profile and no multi-consumer need - see Good vs. Bad Architecture.

Discipline · 04

The C4 Model

C4 (Context, Container, Component, Code) is a set of nested diagrams at increasing zoom levels. It solves the classic problem of architecture diagrams: either too abstract to be useful, or too detailed to see the shape of the system.

The four C4 levels (the fourth is rarely needed)
LevelShowsAudienceQuestion it answers
1. ContextYour system as one box, its users, and the other systems it talks toExecutives, other teams, auditors"What is this thing and what does it touch?"
2. ContainerThe deployable units inside your system (services, apps, databases, queues)Architects, tech leads across teams"What are the moving parts and how do they talk?"
3. ComponentThe internal structure of one containerEngineers building that container"How is this one service organized inside?"
4. CodeClasses/functions inside one componentThe engineer writing it, right nowUsually skipped - the code itself is the diagram

An architect reviewing an AI-generated design should expect Context and Container at minimum, with Component diagrams for any container carrying meaningful internal complexity or risk. The full worked set for ClaimSight - Context, Container, and one Component drill-down - is in Document 2.

Reviewer's checklist for any C4 diagram

Every box has a name and a technology. Every arrow has a label describing what crosses it (a protocol, a data type, an event name) and a direction. External systems are visually distinct from your own containers. Nothing is on the diagram that isn't in the accompanying text, and nothing load-bearing is missing from the diagram.

Discipline · 05

Architecture Decision Records (ADRs)

An ADR is a short, permanent, numbered document that captures one significant decision: the context that forced it, the decision itself, the options that were rejected and why, and the consequences accepted. ADRs are the single most important artifact for evaluating AI-generated architecture, because they force the "why" into writing where it can be checked.

Standard ADR anatomy
SectionContains
StatusProposed / Accepted / Superseded / Rejected
ContextThe forces at play: the requirement, the constraint, the load pattern, the deadline
DecisionThe one sentence that states what was chosen, stated as a commitment, not a suggestion
Rejected AlternativesEvery option seriously considered and not chosen, with the specific reason it lost
ConsequencesWhat this decision makes easier, what it makes harder, and what it costs

The tell that an AI (or a person) skipped the hard part

If a design document states a decision but lists no rejected alternatives, treat it as unreviewed. A decision with no rejected options usually means no real comparison happened - or the comparison happened silently and can't be audited later. Send it back and ask: "what did you consider and rule out, and why?"

Seven real ADRs for ClaimSight - covering the event backbone, the fraud model sourcing strategy, OCR/document intelligence, legacy mainframe integration, deployment topology, and API style - are in Document 3. Each one follows this exact anatomy.

Discipline · 06

Trade-off Analysis

There is no free-lunch technology choice. An architect's value is in stating the cost of a decision as clearly as the benefit. A trade-off matrix scores real options against weighted criteria so the decision is auditable instead of a gut call dressed up after the fact.

How to run one

  1. List real options - at least two genuine alternatives, not one option and two straw men.
  2. Weight the criteria - not every quality attribute matters equally for this decision; say so with numbers.
  3. Score honestly - including scoring your preferred option's weaknesses. A matrix where the "winner" scores highest on every row is a sign the scoring was reverse-engineered from a conclusion already reached.
  4. State the cost of the winner, not just the benefit - every ADR's Consequences section should trace back to this.

Reviewer's red flag

A trade-off matrix with only wins and no losses for the recommended option is not analysis, it is advocacy. Real trade-offs cost something. If nothing was given up, the criteria were probably chosen after the answer.

Two full worked matrices - event backbone selection and OCR/document-intelligence vendor selection - with weighted scoring and a chart, are in Document 5.

Discipline · 07

Key Patterns Worth Recognizing

You do not need to be able to implement these from memory. You need to recognize when an AI-generated design is missing one it needs.

Patterns that show up constantly in enterprise AI system design
PatternWhat it solves
Anti-Corruption LayerIsolates your clean domain model from a legacy or external system's messy one, so the legacy system's constraints don't leak into every service that touches it. Used for ClaimSight's mainframe integration (ADR-005).
Circuit BreakerStops calling a failing downstream dependency after N consecutive failures, so your system fails fast and predictably instead of hanging and cascading the failure upward.
Strangler FigIncrementally replaces a legacy system by routing an increasing share of traffic to the new system, without a risky big-bang cutover.
Event Sourcing / Append-only LogStores every state-changing event permanently instead of only current state, enabling replay, audit reconstruction, and multiple independent consumers of the same history.
Idempotency KeyMakes an operation safe to retry - the same request submitted twice produces the same end state, not a duplicate side effect. Non-negotiable for any retried or replayed operation.
Dead-Letter QueueCatches messages that fail processing after retries are exhausted, so they are triaged manually instead of silently dropped or infinitely retried.
BulkheadIsolates resource pools (thread pools, connection pools) between components so one overloaded component cannot starve the whole system.
Human-in-the-Loop GateForces a human decision point before a high-consequence automated action executes - e.g. a fraud-flagged claim cannot auto-settle.
Discipline · 08

Fitness Functions

A fitness function is an automated, repeatable check that a specific architectural characteristic still holds - run continuously, not just reviewed once at design time. It turns an architectural intention ("we will not violate data residency") into a test that fails the build if violated.

Example fitness functions relevant to ClaimSight
Architectural intentAutomated check
No service directly calls PolicyCore's SOAP endpointStatic import-graph scan fails the build if any module outside the Legacy Integration Adapter imports the SOAP client
All claims data for the 3 residency-restricted states stays in continental US regionsNightly infrastructure scan of region tags on every data store and compute resource; alerts on any non-US-region resource touching claims data
Every external call has a bounded timeoutStatic analysis rule that flags any outbound HTTP client instantiated without an explicit timeout parameter
Kafka consumer lag stays inside the CAT-event SLAContinuous metric threshold alert on consumer group lag vs. autoscaling target

Why this matters for AI-generated code

An AI agent will not remember an architectural rule from three sprints ago unless something enforces it mechanically. Fitness functions are how an architect's decision survives contact with a thousand future code-generation sessions.

Discipline · 09

Build vs. Buy

The default question for any non-differentiating capability should be "why would we build this?" not "why would we buy it?" Building is justified when the capability is core to competitive advantage, needs tuning no vendor offers, or a suitable vendor genuinely does not exist.

A simple decision frame
SignalLeans BuyLeans Build
Is this differentiating for the business?No - it's table stakes (OCR, auth, payments)Yes - it's the actual product advantage
Time-to-value pressureHigh - a deadline the org cannot moveLow - time to iterate exists
Data/domain specificity neededLow - generic capability worksHigh - needs proprietary data or rules no vendor has
Long-run cost at scaleAcceptable, or vendor pricing scales sub-linearlyVendor pricing would become punitive at your volume

ClaimSight makes two build-vs-buy calls worth studying because neither is a clean "buy" or "build" - both land on a deliberate hybrid, which is often the mature answer: see ADR-003 (fraud scoring: buy now, build a challenger in parallel) and ADR-004 (document intelligence: buy the OCR engine, build a thin domain-specific layer on top) in Document 3.

Discipline · 10

Good vs. Bad Architecture

Good architecture is not the most sophisticated one. It is the one that meets the measured requirements with the least accidental complexity, and says so in writing.

Signals to look for in a review
SignalGood architectureBad architecture
Complexity vs. loadEvent-driven backbone justified by a documented 46x spike requirementMicroservices and message queues for an internal tool with 20 daily users
DecisionsWritten ADRs with rejected alternatives and stated costsChoices embedded silently in code with no record of what was considered
NFRsEvery quality attribute has a number and a measurement method"The system should be fast and secure"
Legacy integrationAnti-corruption layer isolates legacy constraints from the new systemEvery new service calls the legacy SOAP endpoint directly
Failure handlingExplicit timeout, retry policy, circuit breaker, and dead-letter path for every external callBare try/catch that swallows the exception and moves on
Vendor dependencyData portability and an exit path are contractually and architecturally consideredCore business logic locked entirely inside a single vendor's proprietary format

Over-engineering is under-engineering with better marketing

Both failure modes have the same root cause: the design was not actually derived from the measured NFRs. One overshoots them for the sake of looking modern; the other undershoots them because nobody measured. The fix is the same in both directions - trace every structural decision back to a number.

Discipline · 11

Architecture KPIs

Architecture quality is not just judged at design time - it is measured continuously once the system is live. These are the KPIs an architect tracks to know whether the design is holding up in production.

Standard architecture health KPIs
KPIWhat it tells youHealthy direction
Deployment frequencyHow easily the architecture allows safe, independent changeHigher, with stable change failure rate
Change failure rateWhether the system's contracts and tests actually catch breakageLower
Mean time to recovery (MTTR)Whether observability and failure isolation are workingLower
Uptime %Whether availability NFRs are being met in practiceAt or above the stated NFR target
Defect escape rateWhether the test pyramid is catching issues before productionLower
Technical debt ratioWhether the codebase is staying within its size/complexity ceilingsStable or improving, not compounding
Cost per transactionWhether the architecture is economically sustainable at scaleFlat or decreasing as volume grows
ADR compliance rateWhether the codebase still matches its own written decisionsHigh - drift here means the ADRs are stale or being ignored
Fitness function pass rateWhether automated architectural guardrails are holding100%, with fast triage on any failure
Discipline · 12

The Architect's Review Lens

This is the checklist to run whenever an AI system - or anyone else - hands you a design and asks for a decision. It compresses everything above into one pass.

  1. Does every quality attribute have a number? If not, it is not reviewable - send it back with the specific missing NFRs named.
  2. Is the architecture style justified against the actual load pattern? Ask for the load numbers, not the adjective.
  3. Does every significant decision have an ADR with rejected alternatives? No rejected options = no real comparison happened.
  4. Does the trade-off analysis state costs, not just wins? An all-upside matrix is advocacy, not analysis.
  5. Is legacy/external integration isolated behind an anti-corruption layer, or does the legacy system's mess leak into the new design?
  6. Does every external call have a timeout, retry policy, and failure path? Silent failure handling is a production defect waiting to happen.
  7. Is every side-effecting operation idempotent? "Works once" is not "works."
  8. Are compliance and data-residency constraints named explicitly, with an enforcement mechanism, not just a mention in a paragraph?
  9. Is there a fitness function or test for each non-negotiable architectural rule, so the rule survives future changes automatically?
  10. Would a competent engineer who was not in the room understand why each decision was made? If the reasoning only lives in someone's head, it does not count as decided.

This is the lens applied throughout the rest of this guide

Every document from here forward - the SAD, the C4 diagrams, the ADRs, the NFR spec, the trade-off matrix, the sequence diagrams, the risk register, and the sign-off package - was built to survive exactly this ten-point review.

Worked Example

Meridian Mutual Insurance & the ClaimSight Initiative

Every document from here forward is built against one concrete, believable company and one concrete initiative. Nothing below is generic - it is what a real solution architect would produce for this specific business problem.

The company

Meridian Mutual Insurance - a mid-size, mutually-owned Property & Casualty carrier headquartered in Columbus, Ohio. Founded 1962.

  • 1.85 million policyholders across 14 Midwest and Southeast states
  • $2.1B annual written premium
  • 3,150 employees, including 1,240 claims adjusters (field and desk)
  • Lines of business: Homeowners, Auto, Renters, Small Commercial

The initiative

ClaimSight - an AI-powered claims intake, triage, and fraud-risk scoring platform. Sponsor: Denise Okafor, SVP Claims Operations.

Business requirements are already approved by Claims Operations leadership. This guide starts at the architecture step: turning those ten approved requirements into a system design.

1. Purpose & Scope

This document defines the target-state solution architecture for ClaimSight, the AI-powered claims intake, triage, and fraud-risk scoring platform commissioned by Meridian Mutual Insurance's Claims Operations division. It is the reference architecture used by engineering to build the system, by security and compliance to review it, and by regulatory examiners to understand automated decisioning.

In scope: FNOL intake (web/mobile/call), document and photo processing, fraud-risk scoring, automated triage and lane routing, the adjuster workbench, and all integration with PolicyCore, NICB ISO ClaimSearch, and the payment disbursement system.

Out of scope: Changes to PolicyCore itself (explicitly prohibited per BR-5), the payment disbursement engine's internal logic, and Meridian's underwriting/quoting systems.

3. Architectural Drivers

Four requirements structurally shape this architecture more than any others: BR-6 (46x CAT-event load spike) forces an elastic, event-driven core rather than fixed-capacity request/response. BR-5 (no changes to PolicyCore) forces an anti-corruption layer rather than direct point-to-point integration. BR-4 (60-second fraud scoring) forces asynchronous, low-latency ML inference in the critical path rather than batch scoring. BR-10 (full decision audit trail) forces an append-only, independently queryable audit log rather than mutable state alone. The full derivation of every measurable target is in the NFR Specification (Document 4).

4. Solution Overview

ClaimSight is an event-driven microservices architecture with synchronous REST APIs only at its two human-facing edges (the FNOL Portal and the Adjuster Workbench). An Apache Kafka event backbone connects Claims Intake, Document Intelligence, Fraud Risk Scoring, and the Triage & Routing Engine, allowing each to scale independently and absorb the 46x CAT-event spike without back-pressuring the FNOL Portal. Legacy integration with PolicyCore is isolated behind a dedicated Legacy Integration Adapter acting as an anti-corruption layer. See ADR-001 for the full justification and rejected alternatives.

5. Data Architecture

Claim state is owned by the Claims Data Store (PostgreSQL) and is never written directly by more than one service - the Claims Intake Service is the sole writer of core claim records, with all other services updating claim state exclusively via events consumed back into it. Documents and photos are stored as immutable objects, referenced by ID from claim records, never embedded. The Audit & Compliance Log Store is append-only by design - no service holds UPDATE or DELETE grants on it, satisfying BR-10 and BR-8 (7-year retention with legal-hold override) by construction rather than by policy alone.

6. Integration Architecture

Three external integrations matter architecturally. PolicyCore (policy verification, coverage limits) is reached only through the Legacy Integration Adapter, which translates PolicyCore's SOAP interface (rate-limited to 4 concurrent connections) and nightly batch file exchange into internal domain events - see ADR-005 and Risk R-01. NICB ISO ClaimSearch (cross-carrier fraud consortium data) is called by the Fraud Risk Scoring Service with a circuit breaker and a 5-second timeout; a NICB outage degrades fraud scoring to claim-history-only signals rather than blocking triage. Payment Disbursement System receives approved Fast-Track claim payments via an idempotent, keyed API call (idempotency key = claim ID + settlement version) to guarantee no duplicate payment on retry.

7. Security & Compliance Architecture

All claims data is encrypted at rest (AES-256) and in transit (TLS 1.2+). Role-based access control gates every Adjuster Workbench and administrative endpoint, scoped so an adjuster can only act on claims assigned to their book of business; SIU investigators have elevated read access to fraud-scoring rationale. Data-residency for the 3 constrained states is enforced structurally by deploying all claims-data-bearing resources exclusively in continental US regions, verified by an automated fitness function (see Fitness Functions). Full compliance targets are itemized in the NFR Specification.

8. Deployment & Infrastructure

Single-region active deployment (US-East) with a warm-standby disaster-recovery environment in a second US region (US-West), per ADR-006. Claims Intake and Document Intelligence services autoscale horizontally from a steady-state of 4 replicas to a CAT-event ceiling of 120 replicas, triggered by Kafka consumer-lag and queue-depth metrics with a target scale-up time under 5 minutes. Deployment topology, sequencing, and failure paths are detailed in Document 6.

9. Key Architectural Decisions (Summary)

Seven decisions carry the structural weight of this architecture, each fully recorded with rejected alternatives in Document 3: event-driven architecture over a synchronous monolith (ADR-001); Kafka as the event backbone (ADR-002); a hybrid buy-then-build strategy for fraud scoring (ADR-003); buy-managed-OCR-plus-build-custom-NLP for document intelligence (ADR-004); an anti-corruption layer for PolicyCore integration (ADR-005); single-region active/passive deployment (ADR-006); and REST over GraphQL for the Adjuster Workbench API (ADR-007).

10. Risks & Open Items

The ten highest-priority architectural risks - including PolicyCore's SOAP connection ceiling under CAT-event load and fraud-model false-positive rate - are tracked with owners and mitigations in the Architecture Risk Register (Document 7).

ADR-001

Adopt Event-Driven Architecture Over a Synchronous Monolith

Accepted
Context

BR-6 requires ClaimSight to sustain 46x average daily claim volume for 72 continuous hours following a landfall hurricane or derecho, without degrading FNOL submission below a 99.5% success rate. A fixed-capacity, synchronously-coupled system sized for average load cannot absorb this spike without either massive permanent over-provisioning or dropped requests during the exact week claims volume - and public trust - matters most.

Decision

ClaimSight's core is event-driven: services publish and consume domain events over a durable event bus, each scaling independently. Synchronous REST is used only at the two human-facing edges (FNOL Portal, Adjuster Workbench) where a request/response interaction is the natural fit.

Rejected Alternatives
Monolithic synchronous REST-only architecture - simplest to build and operate, but a single deployable cannot scale FNOL intake independently from fraud scoring; a slow downstream component blocks the whole request chain exactly when load is highest.
Modular monolith with vertical autoscaling only - improves on a pure monolith, but vertical scaling has a ceiling and does not solve independent scaling of Document Intelligence (CPU/GPU-bound) versus Claims Intake (I/O-bound), which have very different resource profiles under CAT load.
Consequences

Gains: independent, elastic scaling per service; durable event replay for audit reconstruction (supports BR-10); natural multi-consumer fan-out (Analytics Data Mart consumes the same events as Triage & Routing). Costs: eventual consistency between services; higher operational complexity than a monolith; requires the team to build competence in event schema versioning and consumer-group management.

ADR-002

Apache Kafka as the Event Backbone

Accepted
Context

Having chosen an event-driven core (ADR-001), ClaimSight needs a specific event backbone technology that supports high-throughput bursts, long retention for regulatory reconstruction of triage decisions, and replay by multiple independent consumers (Triage Engine, Analytics Data Mart, Audit Log Writer) without those consumers competing for the same messages.

Decision

Apache Kafka (managed/MSK) is the event backbone for all inter-service communication, with a minimum 30-day retention window on claim-lifecycle topics and replication factor 3 / minimum in-sync replicas 2.

Rejected Alternatives
RabbitMQ - excellent for task-queue-style workloads, but its retention model is built around message consumption/deletion rather than durable replay; reconstructing "what did the Triage Engine know when it made this decision" 90 days later for a regulatory examiner is awkward and not RabbitMQ's design center.
AWS SNS/SQS - simple, fully managed, low ops burden, and adequate for basic fan-out. Rejected because SQS retention caps at 14 days (short of what a regulatory reconstruction scenario may need) and multi-consumer replay of the same message stream requires bolting SNS fan-out onto multiple SQS queues, adding complexity without matching Kafka's native consumer-group replay model.
Consequences

Gains: long retention supports audit reconstruction; native consumer groups let Analytics and Audit consume independently without interfering with Triage; proven at CAT-scale throughput. Costs: Kafka has real operational surface area (partition sizing, consumer lag monitoring) even when managed; the team carries a genuine learning curve versus a simpler queue.

ADR-003

Fraud-Risk Scoring: Hybrid Buy-Then-Build Strategy

Accepted
Context

BR-4 requires fraud-risk scoring within 60 seconds of intake, live at launch. A competitive, well-tuned fraud model normally takes 12-18 months to build in-house from a carrier's own claims history - longer than ClaimSight's launch timeline allows - yet a purely bought model can never be tuned to Meridian's specific regional fraud patterns, and permanent vendor dependency for a capability this close to core underwriting risk is a real long-term concern.

Decision

License a vendor fraud-scoring engine for year one to meet the launch deadline, while building an in-house challenger model in parallel on Meridian's own claims history. Cut over to the in-house model per line of business only once it out-performs the vendor model on backtested precision/recall for two consecutive quarters.

Rejected Alternatives
Pure buy (permanent vendor dependency) - fastest to launch, but locks fraud-detection quality to the vendor's cross-industry model, which cannot learn Meridian's specific regional and line-of-business fraud patterns, and creates renewal-leverage risk once the business depends on it (see Risk R-05).
Pure build from day one - best long-run fit and no vendor lock-in, but a 12-18 month build timeline directly conflicts with BR-1/BR-4's launch expectations; Claims Operations leadership will not accept a year-plus delay to FNOL time reduction.
Consequences

Gains: meets the launch deadline without permanently accepting vendor dependency; the in-house challenger accumulates Meridian-specific training data from day one, shortening the eventual cutover. Costs: running two scoring paths in parallel during the transition period is real engineering and evaluation overhead; requires disciplined quarterly precision/recall review (see NFR spec) to avoid an indefinite "always almost ready" in-house model.

ADR-004

Document Intelligence: Buy Managed OCR, Build Custom NLP Layer

Accepted
Context

BR-3 requires ≥95% field-level extraction accuracy across 12 core claim fields from photos, PDFs, scanned police reports, and call transcriptions. Raw OCR/document-AI is a mature, commoditized capability; insurance-specific field normalization (peril codes, VIN parsing, address matching against PolicyCore) is not something any generic vendor product does out of the box.

Decision

License a managed cloud Document AI / OCR service for raw text and layout extraction. Build a thin, in-house NLP normalization layer on top that maps raw extraction into Meridian's 12 core claim fields and validates them against PolicyCore reference data.

Rejected Alternatives
Full in-house OCR/NLP build - would give complete control, but recreating mature, well-trained OCR is undifferentiated heavy lifting for Meridian; it would consume most of the Phase 1 timeline on a capability multiple vendors already solve well, and would very likely under-perform vendor OCR on messy handwritten police reports at launch.
Consequences

Gains: reaches the 95% accuracy target inside the Phase 1 timeline by relying on mature vendor OCR; engineering effort concentrates on the genuinely differentiating normalization layer. Costs: per-page vendor OCR cost scales with claim volume (a real line item during CAT-event spikes, tracked in the cost-per-claim KPI); a second point of vendor dependency alongside the fraud-scoring vendor.

ADR-005

Anti-Corruption Layer for PolicyCore Integration

Accepted
Context

BR-5 prohibits any modification to PolicyCore, a COBOL/CICS mainframe in production since 1998 with a documented 4-concurrent-connection ceiling on its SOAP interface and a nightly batch file-exchange window. Multiple new ClaimSight services need policy and coverage data from PolicyCore.

Decision

All PolicyCore access is routed through a single Legacy Integration Adapter service that translates PolicyCore's SOAP and batch interfaces into internal domain events and APIs. No other ClaimSight service calls PolicyCore directly - enforced by a fitness function (see Discipline · Fitness Functions).

Rejected Alternatives
Direct point-to-point SOAP calls from every service that needs policy data - simplest to code per-service, but multiple concurrent ClaimSight services calling PolicyCore directly would immediately exceed its 4-connection ceiling during any real load, let alone a CAT-event spike, and spreads PolicyCore's legacy quirks (COBOL date formats, fixed-width fields) across the whole codebase.
Rip-and-replace of PolicyCore - would remove the constraint entirely, but is explicitly out of scope per BR-5, is a 25+ year system of record with enormous blast radius, and would constitute the kind of large-scale rewrite that requires executive escalation under this program's governance model, not a ClaimSight-scoped decision.
Consequences

Gains: PolicyCore's constraints and quirks are contained in one service instead of leaking everywhere; the 4-connection ceiling can be actively managed (queuing, batching) in one place. Costs: the Legacy Integration Adapter becomes a single, business-critical dependency and a key-person risk (see Risk R-01, R-07) - it must be built and documented to a higher standard than a typical internal service.

ADR-006

Single-Region Active/Passive Deployment Topology

Accepted
Context

ClaimSight needs a disaster-recovery posture proportionate to Meridian's current scale ($2.1B written premium, 14 states) without over-building for a size the business has not reached.

Decision

Deploy ClaimSight active in a single US region (US-East) with a warm-standby disaster-recovery environment in a second US region (US-West). Target RTO 4 hours, RPO 15 minutes.

Rejected Alternatives
Multi-region active-active - would provide near-zero RTO/RPO and geographic load distribution, but costs roughly 2.4x the single-region topology and introduces cross-region data-consistency complexity (conflict resolution on claim writes) that is not justified at current volume. Revisit if written premium exceeds $3.5B or Meridian expands into a second CAT-prone region.
Consequences

Gains: meaningfully lower infrastructure cost than active-active; simpler consistency model (single writer region at any time). Costs: a 4-hour RTO means up to 4 hours of degraded service in a full regional failure - acceptable per current risk appetite, but must be explicitly re-confirmed with Claims Operations leadership, not assumed.

ADR-007

REST/JSON Over GraphQL for the Adjuster Workbench API

Accepted
Context

The Adjuster Workbench needs an API contract for the API Gateway. Query patterns are well-known in advance (claim detail, claim list by adjuster book, triage history) rather than open-ended and client-driven.

Decision

Expose REST/JSON endpoints with an OpenAPI specification as the contract, satisfying the Contract Enforcement expectations for typed, testable API surfaces.

Rejected Alternatives
GraphQL - would give the Adjuster Workbench flexible, client-driven queries, but the query patterns here are already known and stable, the team has no existing GraphQL operational experience, and GraphQL's N+1 resolver risk adds a failure mode with no offsetting benefit for this specific, well-understood access pattern.
Consequences

Gains: simpler caching, simpler contract testing, faster onboarding for engineers already fluent in REST. Costs: if the Adjuster Workbench's query needs become significantly more dynamic in the future, REST endpoints may proliferate; revisit if endpoint count exceeds roughly 40 for this container.
