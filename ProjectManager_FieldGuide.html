Week 3 Deep Dive · Discipline: Project Manager

Direct. Evaluate. Approve.

You are not becoming a project manager. You are becoming the AI Solution Architect who directs an AI-generated project plan, evaluates whether it is sound, and approves it before it touches production, budget, or a regulator. This guide is the ~20% of project management discipline that makes that judgment defensible: the triangle, the WBS, the critical path, RAID, RACI, and the eight documents every AI-built delivery plan should produce — plus a worked, end-to-end example so the theory has somewhere to live.

Why Project Management Exists

An AI model can draft a plan in seconds. It cannot be held accountable for the plan being right. Project management exists to convert intent into commitments that specific, named people can be held to — and to make the gap between the plan and reality visible before it becomes expensive.

Three failure modes show up constantly when AI-generated plans go unreviewed: optimism bias (every estimate is a best case with no buffer), ownership diffusion (a risk everyone owns is a risk no one owns), and invisible dependency (a task that looks parallel but secretly isn't). Project management is the set of habits — decomposing scope, sequencing dependencies, naming one owner per risk, reporting status against a baseline — that catch these three failure modes before they become incidents. As an AI Solution Architect, you don't need the full PMBOK. You need the 20% of the discipline that lets you look at an AI-drafted plan and know, quickly and defensibly, whether to approve it.

The Fixed Triangle: Scope, Schedule, Cost

Every project has three levers — scope (what gets built), schedule (when), and cost (what it takes) — constrained by a fourth, non-negotiable variable: quality. You can hold at most two of scope, schedule, and cost fixed. The third must flex. A plan that claims all three are fixed and non-negotiable is not a plan — it's a wish.

ClaimSight AI's constraint
Budget ($1,240,000) and schedule (pilot go-live must land before Meridian's next state DOI filing cycle) are fixed. Scope is the flex variable — which is why the charter explicitly limits phase one to the Auto claims line in a single pilot region, with Property/Commercial lines deferred to a phase two decision.

When you review an AI-generated plan, the first question is not "is this achievable?" — it's "which variable is this plan quietly flexing, and did anyone approve that?" AI planning tools routinely hold all three fixed by under-scoping risk or over-compressing estimates. That's the tell to look for.

Work Breakdown Structure (WBS)

A WBS decomposes total project scope into work packages small enough to estimate, assign, and track — and the work packages must sum to 100% of scope, no more, no less. This is the single most useful sanity check an architect can run on an AI-generated plan: ask for the WBS as a percentage breakdown and add it up. If it doesn't sum to 100%, either scope is missing (the plan will blow through budget silently) or scope is double-counted (the estimate is inflated).

Rule of thumb: the 8/80 rule
A healthy work package takes between 8 and 80 hours (roughly one day to two weeks) for one owner to complete. Smaller, and the tracking overhead exceeds the value; larger, and it hides schedule risk until it's too late to react.

See the full ClaimSight AI WBS — seven phases, twenty-one work packages, summing to exactly 100% — in Document 2, including the WBS tree diagram.

Critical Path & Float

The critical path is the longest chain of dependent tasks through the project — it determines the earliest possible finish date. Any slip on the critical path slips the whole project, day for day. Tasks not on the critical path carry float (or "slack"): the amount of time they can slip without affecting the finish date.

The counter-intuitive part — and it happens in ClaimSight AI
The critical path is not always the biggest or most visible task. In the ClaimSight AI timeline (Document 3), the Intake Extraction Engine (the bigger build, 20% of WBS effort) is not on the critical path — it has 4 weeks of float. The Triage & Routing Engine, which starts later and looks smaller in the room, has zero float and is what actually drives the finish date, because Integration cannot start until both are done and Triage finishes later. An architect who only asks "what's the biggest workstream?" will protect the wrong thing.

Practical rule for review: ask the plan's author (human or AI) to name the critical path explicitly and show the float on every non-critical task. A plan with no stated critical path has not actually been sequenced — it's a task list wearing a Gantt chart's clothes.

Milestones & Gates

A milestone marks a point in time (a phase finishing, a deliverable landing). A gate is a milestone with teeth: it has explicit, written exit criteria that must be met before work is authorized to proceed, and a named decision-maker who approves passage. Gates are where an architect's approval authority actually lives — everything before the gate is drafting; the gate itself is a decision.

RAID: Risks, Assumptions, Issues, Dependencies

RRisks

Something that might happen and would hurt the project if it did. Scored by likelihood × impact. Every risk needs a named owner and a mitigation — "monitor" is not a mitigation.

AAssumptions

Something believed true but not verified, which the plan depends on. Assumptions are risks in disguise — write them down so they can be tested, not discovered.

IIssues

A risk that already happened. It has stopped being a probability and started being a fact that needs a resolution date, not a mitigation plan.

DDependencies

Something the project needs from outside its own control — another team, a vendor, an approval. The project cannot resolve its own dependencies; it can only track them and escalate when they slip.

Rule: one owner per row, always
A RAID row with no owner, or with "team" as the owner, will not get resolved. See Document 4 for the live ClaimSight AI RAID log — including R2/I1, a risk that matured into an issue mid-project, which is exactly what a good RAID log is supposed to catch.

RACI Matrix

RACI assigns one of four roles to every person against every activity:

  • A  Accountable — owns the outcome. Exactly one per activity, no exceptions.
  • R  Responsible — does the work. Can be the same person as Accountable.
  • C  Consulted — two-way input before the decision.
  • I  Informed — told after the decision, one-way.
The rule that matters most
Exactly one Accountable per row. Two Accountables means no one is actually accountable — each will assume the other is covering it. This is the single fastest check an architect can run on an AI-generated RACI matrix: scan the A column per activity; if any row has zero or more than one, reject it.

Agile, Waterfall, and Hybrid

ModelBest fitWeakness
WaterfallFixed regulatory scope, low ambiguity, hardware/compliance-gated deliveryExpensive to change direction once building has started
Agile (Scrum)High ambiguity, evolving requirements, iterative model/software buildNeeds a stable team and a product owner who can decide fast
HybridRegulated delivery with an uncertain technical core — most enterprise AI initiativesRequires discipline to keep the waterfall gates from swallowing the agile cadence

ClaimSight AI runs hybrid: the seven-phase, gated timeline (Document 3) is waterfall-shaped because it must land before a fixed regulatory filing window — but the two build phases (Intake Extraction, Triage & Routing) run in two-week Scrum sprints internally, because model accuracy work is inherently iterative. Document 6 (Sprint Plan) is the agile layer living inside the waterfall shell. This is the most common real-world pattern for enterprise AI delivery, and it's what "hybrid" should mean in practice — not a vague middle ground, but a deliberate choice of which layer gets which model.

Velocity & Burndown

Velocity is the average story points a team completes per sprint, measured over several sprints — it's a planning input, not a performance score. A burndown chart plots remaining work (points) against time within a single sprint: an "ideal" line assuming steady progress, against the "actual" line showing what really happened.

What a burndown that lags the ideal line actually means
It does not automatically mean the team is underperforming. It means committed scope should be re-examined against the sprint goal — descope, add help, or accept the sprint will finish late. See Document 6 for ClaimSight AI's Sprint 6 burndown, which is running behind ideal for a specific, named reason (a delayed labeled dataset — the same issue tracked as I1 in the RAID log).

Committing above the trailing average velocity without a stated reason is a red flag in any AI-generated sprint plan — it usually means the plan is optimizing for looking ambitious rather than being deliverable.

Status Reporting & RAG Health

RAG (Red / Amber / Green) status communicates project health at a glance, but it is only useful if it is measured against a baseline, not against a feeling. Green means on baseline. Amber means at risk of missing baseline without intervention — and intervention is already underway. Red means baseline has been missed or will be without an escalated decision.

The most common status-report failure
A status report that is Green every week until it is suddenly Red. That pattern means status was being reported optimistically, not honestly — amber exists precisely to be the early warning that prevents this. See ClaimSight AI's current status (Document 7): Amber overall, with the specific workstream and root cause named, is a healthy report, not a bad one.

KPIs That Matter

Track the smallest set of metrics that would actually change a decision. For an AI delivery initiative, that generally means: a model quality metric (accuracy/precision against a holdout set), a schedule metric (SPI — Schedule Performance Index), a cost metric (CPI — Cost Performance Index), and a business outcome metric (the thing the project was funded to move — here, intake-to-assignment time). See the six live KPI tiles in Document 7.

SPI / CPI, in one line each
SPI = Earned Value ÷ Planned Value — under 1.0 means behind schedule. CPI = Earned Value ÷ Actual Cost — under 1.0 means over budget for the value delivered. Both are read against governance thresholds (Document 7 uses ≥0.95 Green, 0.85–0.95 Amber, <0.85 Red), not against zero.

The Architect's Review Lens

Seven questions to run against any AI-generated project plan before approving it. This is the practical payoff of everything above.

  1. Which side of the triangle is actually flexing? — and was that approved, or assumed?
  2. Does the WBS sum to 100%? — if not, scope is missing or double-counted.
  3. Is the critical path named, with float shown elsewhere? — not just implied by bar order.
  4. Does every RAID row have one named owner and a date? — "team" is not an owner.
  5. Does every RACI row have exactly one Accountable? — zero or multiple is an automatic reject.
  6. Do gates have written exit criteria and a named decision-maker? — not just a date.
  7. Do estimates carry a stated buffer, and does committed sprint scope respect trailing velocity?
If all seven pass
Approve, log any assumptions you made in the review, and move on — this is what "proceed by default" looks like at the plan-approval layer, not just the code layer.

2. Business Problem

Meridian Mutual's Auto claims adjusters currently spend an average of 46 minutes per claim on manual FNOL transcription, initial severity classification, and fraud screening before a claim is even assigned to an adjuster. During catastrophe events, this creates intake backlogs of 3–5 business days. Fraud-signal screening today is inconsistent across adjusters and channels, and is not applied uniformly to phone-intake claims, which make up 38% of FNOL volume.

3. Objectives

  • Reduce average intake-to-assignment time from 46 minutes to under 12 minutes for Auto claims in the pilot region.
  • Increase early fraud-signal flag detection rate by at least 30% relative to the current manual baseline.
  • Maintain claim-cycle Net Promoter Score at or above its current baseline throughout pilot (no customer-experience regression).
  • Keep a licensed adjuster as the accountable, human-in-the-loop decision-maker on every claim — ClaimSight AI recommends, it never assigns or denies autonomously.

4. Scope Boundary

In scope

  • Multi-channel FNOL ingestion: web form, email, and phone-transcript intake
  • Structured data extraction from unstructured intake content
  • Severity and fraud-signal scoring, presented as an adjuster recommendation
  • Adjuster assignment/routing recommendation
  • Pilot deployment: Auto claims line, Southeast territory only

Out of scope (this phase)

  • Full claims adjudication or autonomous claim decisioning
  • Property and Commercial lines (candidate for a separately chartered Phase 2)
  • Any change to adjuster decision authority — recommendations remain advisory
  • Catastrophe-event surge automation (handled by existing CAT playbooks)
